## The Mastery of the Visible and Invisible

*Field Notes on How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love Weird Knowledge*

Where does an interest in photography begin? For most, it starts and ends with the desire to show reality as it is. My camera began as a simple mirror, but it became a scalpel for dissecting the visible surface, for laying bare the hidden stories, textures, and meanings frozen in time.

## The Mirror Became a Scalpel

It all started with knights in the Vyborg castle. I was given a Soviet Zenit camera, and then a picture was published in the local newspaper with the caption: “Photojournalist Alexander Korolev.” I learned the basics of photojournalism and documentary photography. I photographed a lot of old ladies selling balloons near the metro, police beating people at rallies, and people hiding from the police in remote forests outside the city. With such work, I quickly realized that shooting news was useless because nothing new was happening in the world. Now, people like me are easily replaced by random passersby with smartphones.

## Darkroom Lessons

Then I started teaching in a student darkroom lab, and five years there flew by. The smell of fixer on your fingers, white developer stains on your clothes, dust on the film... Film teaches you to value every shot? Nonsense. The only thing the darkroom actually teaches you is that “documentary truth” in photography is just another convention. Lith printing adds chance to this convention — each subsequent print as unique as providence itself.

> “The best thing about a picture is that it never changes, even when the people in it do.” — Andy Warhol

## The Archaeological Turn

Sometimes I would emerge from the darkroom to photograph my first museum projects and learn a little art history with other “eternal” students. I started out documenting exhibits for catalogs and covering openings, and ended up documenting the full cycle of an art exhibition as a statement — from prep to finishing and dismantling.

One time, I went on an archaeological expedition, where I learned firsthand that all the hidden stories of antiquity are essentially hours and days of excavation, measuring, and clearing dust under the scorching sun. And someday, you’ll understand what objects you’ve found and what their purpose was. Photographic “interdisciplinary vision” became a record of my sensations from what I saw in the past and present, from images that attest to both past existence and temporal distance. Everything became an expedition document: artifact inventories, ceramic heaps, terrain views, the daily life, labor, and triumph of the participants. The art of photography finally became for me the deformation of the ordinary into the mysterious and multilayered, accompanied, when necessary, by scientific explanation.

The archaeological project became an excellent pretext for a series of expeditions to places rich in history and, as a rule, in problems: Lebanon, Syria, Egypt, Central Asia. This experience truly enriched my understanding of how photography can be a powerful tool for studying civilizational heritage, both living and man-made. Before it meets the fate of Palmyra…

## No? Future!

In my participation in the Manifesta I started making contemporary art because I didn’t believe in its modernity, only in its artificiality. After a brief discussion in the kitchen, Jordi Colomer convinced me to work on the “No? Future!” project with joy and skepticism.

In the final year of my master’s program, I tackled the problem of intellectual regional studies of St. Petersburg using R and QGIS, gained invaluable experience driving a half-dead LADA-2110, and learned the differences between documentary and mockumentary. The performance was accompanied by hot pursuits, forced evacuations, impound lots, and lawsuits.

The apotheosis of this interdisciplinarity was the creation of an exhibition of the final document in the Hermitage Museum. The space for harsh exposition was such that an agreement with our German curator could only be reached with the help of half a liter of “samogonochka” and a lecture on cultural stereotypes…

## The Infrared Turn

After years of documenting the world, I became captivated by the idea of challenging our ways of seeing. Berger taught us that any perspective is a construct. What if I could use a technology that offered a perspective entirely alien to the human eye?

The infrared turn occurred with complex landscapes and shifted to human history in the invisible world. The visible light portrait of a person created (in Barthes’s terminology) a studium — a culturally conditioned interest in the image. But the reflection of faces, skin, clothing, iron, and walls in infrared light created a punctum — an unintended detail that establishes a connection with the “old world” of psychedelics, rock ’n’ roll, and Kodak Aerochrome.

Archaeological landscapes in invisible light became a reflection of the history of the human past. Civilizational heritage, with profound philosophical implications, easily transforms into picturesque piles of stones against a backdrop of endless international considerations.

The infrared turn occurred first with 720nm black-and-white — the classic format that makes white foliage and dark skies. Then came 590nm with its false colors: orange leaves, cyan skies, the full “super color” experience. And finally IR Chrome — digital recreation of Kodak Aerochrome, the military surveillance film from Vietnam era that turned jungles pink and made camouflage visible.

## Modus Ex Machina

In 2021, at the European University in St. Petersburg, we combined art history with machine learning technologies to create the “Art and Artificial Intelligence” lab. A documentary photographer and art historian with a love of adventure became its co-founder and CTO.

Our main project was a neural network proudly named SIMILIS — “Shazam for Pottery Sherds” — that searches for analogies with archaeological finds based on photographs. The first attempt was comical: the most “similar” matches the machine found were a button and a cannonball. But after dozens of iterations, the accuracy of the comparisons increased.

Archaeologists have discovered an unexpected result. Where the machine saw a pattern, the human eye saw the hand of a different creator. Discussing the meanings of “reproduction,” “analogy,” “accuracy,” and “digital compromise,” we found the neural network not a replacement for every expert, but a partner in a new kind of dialogue. Between the rigorous transistor mind and the soul of the historian, only photography will serve as a common language.

But can AI create a “simple photo of a simple person”? The works of Dorothea Lange, Martin Parr, Dmitry Markov will leave AI no chance. Mathematical beauty cannot replace photographic humanism. I suspect that this database and seven neural networks are the most useful things I will leave behind (besides my children and my archive).

## Life After the Black Raven

All my life, I sought the invisible — in the layers of history, in the spectra of light. In May 2025, the invisible found me. The invisible hand of the Russian state security service upended my life in a single day. I had to shut down my lab projects, leave the country, and master the simple craft of a “digital nomad” and “independent researcher.” No one in Russia of 2025 is safe from arbitrariness, violence, terror, and threats from the state — I prefer to think I got off easy for the so-called “discreditation of the army and authorities.”

Now, instead of teaching neural networks, I am teaching myself to finish my PhD and to distill all my years of research and creative searching into a single book about the aesthetics of another vision and photography in invisible light — the best way to add a measure of meaning to a world of chaos and injustice. And, of course, to continue shooting the stories of people in natural and infrared light. My first clients were fellow forced migrants, their stories exceptionally important because they were partially destroyed, hidden, or stripped of legitimacy. Independence has become a truly precious word, and I am incredibly grateful to everyone who helps me in my research and creative work. With cautious optimism, I look to a future full of new ideas, amazing discoveries, and the boundless potential of light, humanism, and technology… and the ability to count and measure it all.

## The Method

I work with full-spectrum converted cameras and a range of infrared filters. Each filter reveals a different aspect of the invisible world: 590nm creates false-color imagery with vivid foliage shifts, 720nm produces dramatic black-and-white photographs, and IR Chrome digitally recreates the false-color aesthetic of Kodak Aerochrome film directly in-camera.

### Equipment

- Full-spectrum converted digital cameras
- Filters: 590nm, 650nm, 720nm, 850nm, IR Chrome
- Infrared-optimized optics
- Specialized color-managed sRGB workflow

If you have a story that needs to be told — personal, professional, or historical — we will conduct a joint investigation and look beyond the known and the suspected.
